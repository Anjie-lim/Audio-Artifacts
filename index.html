Start with the listening method

An artifact is an unwanted sound or signal defect that changes the recording or competes with the intended speech. A sound can be audible without making speech unintelligible; severity matters.

Short discussion

What are you listening for?

In speech annotation, the target is the speaker’s message. An audio artifact is an unwanted sound or recording defect that changes how that message is heard—for example, hiss behind the voice, an echo that blurs a word, a pop caused by air hitting the microphone, or a brief dropout that removes part of a word.

First listen to the clean reference to learn the speaker’s normal sound. Then listen to each modified example and focus on what changed. Ask: What do I hear? Where does it occur? How much does it interfere with the words? Describe the audible evidence rather than guessing the cause. Ordinary breaths, accents, hesitations, and expected environmental sounds are not automatically artifacts; follow the project’s labeling rules.

The speaker should remain easier to hear than the effect. Your task is to detect the artifact without losing track of the spoken message.

Step 1

Identify what changed

Listen for a new layer of sound, a repeated reflection, an abrupt gap, a harsh change in the voice, or a sound that masks a word.

Describe what is audible before guessing its cause.
Step 2

Rate the impact

Ask whether the artifact is faint, noticeable, distracting, or strong enough to obscure speech. The same artifact type can occur at different severity levels.

A faint hiss and an intrusive hiss are both noise, but they do not have the same impact.

Suggested severity scale

Minor

Audible but unobtrusive; speech remains clear.

Moderate

Clearly noticeable and somewhat distracting.

Severe

Competes with speech or masks part of the message.

Background sounds and environmental noise

These sounds sit under or around the target speaker. Listen for whether they are continuous, intermittent, tonal, or speech-like.

Continuous noise

Electronic static / hiss

Broadband shhh or crackly noise mixed under the voice. Faint hiss may be minor; louder hiss can mask consonants.

Listen for a steady noise bed that is not part of the speaker's words.
Low-frequency tone

Electrical hum

A low, steady hum under speech, sometimes with small clicks. It may sound like a motor or electrical interference.

Focus on the low drone behind the voice.
Environmental sound

Wind-like rumble

Low, gusting noise added under the spoken line to demonstrate wind striking or buffeting a microphone.

Listen for low bursts that swell and fade beneath syllables.
Stronger environmental noise

Intrusive wind buffeting

A stronger low-frequency, gusting effect under the same speech. This is a simulation of wind buffeting a microphone, not a field recording.

Notice whether the low bursts begin to compete with the words.
Intrusive noise

Loud hiss / noise

A stronger noise bed that competes with the speech. Listen for how much effort it takes to follow the words.

The key distinction is impact on listening, not merely the presence of noise.
Tonal layer

Music-like background tones

Sustained musical tones play beneath the speech. This is a controlled tonal simulation, not a song recording.

Listen for a continuous tonal layer competing with the voice.
Intermittent

Mechanical beeps

A low hum and short beeps occur during the spoken line, like a device sounding in the background.

Notice the timing of the beeps relative to the words.
Intermittent

Chime-like tones

Short, bright tones interrupt the spoken recording. This demonstrates a tonal environmental sound competing with speech.

The tones are separate from the spoken content.
Overlapping speech

Overlapping speech

A slightly delayed, filtered copy of the same spoken line overlaps the main voice. This is a controlled simulation of competing speech, not a real second speaker.

Listen for the words becoming harder to separate because two versions overlap.
Multiple voices

Speech-like background chatter

Two altered copies of the same spoken line are layered behind the main voice to simulate a busy speech environment. They are not genuine crowd recordings.

Listen for competing speech-like sound behind the primary speaker.

Room acoustics and signal defects

Room reflections can blur speech; signal defects can make the voice harsh, broken, or discontinuous.

Distinct repeat

Echo

A delayed repetition of the voice follows the original speech.

Echo is typically heard as a more distinct repeat.
Blended reflections

Reverberation (reverb)

Reflections linger and blend with the voice, making syllables overlap.

Reverb sounds more continuous than a distinct echo.
Transient noise

Crackles and clicks

Short sharp noises are inserted into the speech recording to demonstrate intermittent artifacts.

Listen for brief events rather than a continuous noise bed.
Loud peaks

Clipping

Loud peaks are hard-clipped in this simulation, making the voice sound harsh or crunchy.

Clipping often becomes more noticeable on louder syllables.
Abrupt gaps

Dropouts

Short sections of the speech are muted, creating unexpected gaps while playback continues.

A dropout removes a segment; the recording continues afterward.
Abrupt ending

Truncation

The recording ends before the spoken utterance is complete.

Truncation cuts the recording off; it is different from a brief dropout followed by more speech.

Speech and processing artifacts

Not every unusual vocal sound is an artifact. Compare the modified recording with the clean reference and label only what the audio supports.

Distortion

Heavy distortion

Strong nonlinear processing makes the speech rough or broken and can reduce clarity.

Focus on whether the voice itself sounds unnaturally altered.
Distortion

Localized distortion / interference

The clean voice briefly drops out while a strongly clipped version replaces it for one segment, then the normal voice returns. This makes the contrast easier to hear.

Do not confuse natural vocal roughness with a signal defect without supporting evidence.
High-frequency emphasis

Harsh sibilance

High frequencies are boosted more strongly to make sibilant consonants sound sharper. This is an exaggerated training simulation and may also brighten other consonants.

Listen for sharp, piercing “s” sounds that stand out unnaturally.
Transient impact

Microphone pop

A sharp low-frequency burst and click occur during speech, simulating a plosive striking the microphone.

A plosive is a normal speech sound; the artifact is the excessive microphone burst.
Breath-like noise

Exaggerated breath noise

Filtered noise swells between phrases to demonstrate breathing or air noise that becomes distracting.

Normal breathing is not automatically an artifact; assess whether it is unusually intrusive.
Processing effect

Robotic / mechanical voice quality

The same speech is strongly pulse-modulated to create an obvious, intentionally mechanical rhythm. This is a demonstration of processed voice quality, not proof of an actual recording fault.

A deliberately processed voice effect is not proof of AI generation or a recording fault.
Multiple artifacts

Combined defects

Several controlled effects are combined to practice identifying the most salient audible issues.

Identify each supported artifact, then apply the project's prioritization rules.
Do not over-label: a natural accent, hesitation, stutter, breath, emotional voice, or ordinary consonant is not automatically an artifact. Use the project taxonomy and label only audible evidence.

Practice and sources

Question 1: Background noise

What is the main issue in this clip?

Correct answer: strong background noise. Listen for a continuous noise bed that competes with the spoken line.

Question 2: Echo versus reverb

Which feature is most noticeable?

Correct answer: a delayed repeat. A distinct repeat is the key cue for echo.

Question 3: Dropout or truncation?

What best describes this recording?

Correct answer: brief dropouts. Speech resumes after the gaps; truncation would end the recording prematurely.

Question 4: Distortion

Which description is best supported?

Correct answer: distortion. The voice itself has an unnaturally rough, processed quality.
